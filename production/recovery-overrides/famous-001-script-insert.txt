There is an important distinction here that makes Amazon's structure easier to understand. Revenue and operating income are not the same thing, and the biggest revenue category is not automatically the most economically important one. Online stores brought in $269.287 billion in 2025, making them the largest individual sales category in Amazon's disclosure. But Amazon also reported enormous service businesses sitting around that store, each monetizing a different part of the same customer and seller ecosystem.

Take third-party seller services. The $172.162 billion Amazon reports in that category is not the total value of products independent merchants sold through the marketplace. It is Amazon's revenue from services such as commissions, fulfillment, and shipping fees. That distinction matters. When an independent merchant makes a sale, Amazon can participate in the economics without owning the inventory being sold. And because independent sellers now account for more than 60% of sales in Amazon's store, the marketplace is not a small feature attached to the retail operation. It is one of the systems at the center of it.

Advertising adds another layer to the same transaction. A seller can pay Amazon to store and fulfill a product, and then pay again to put that product in front of a shopper at the moment that shopper is searching. Amazon reported $68.635 billion in advertising-services sales in 2025. That figure is revenue, not profit, so it would be wrong to treat every advertising dollar as pure margin. But it shows how the company can monetize shopping activity before a customer even presses the buy button. Amazon's move to offer Retail Ad Service to participating retailers also shows that the advertising technology itself can become a product beyond Amazon's own storefront.

Subscriptions create a different kind of connection. Amazon reported $49.619 billion in subscription-services sales in 2025, a category that includes Prime membership fees and certain digital subscriptions. The strategic value is not simply the fee. A subscription gives Amazon another recurring relationship with customers who already use the company's shopping and media ecosystem. It is one more layer tied to the same platform rather than a completely separate audience.

Then compare all of those categories with AWS. AWS generated $128.725 billion in sales in 2025, smaller than online stores and third-party seller services. Yet AWS produced $45.606 billion in segment operating income. Amazon as a whole reported $79.975 billion in operating income. Those numbers should not be confused with net income, and they do not mean the rest of Amazon is unprofitable. What they do show is how unusually important the cloud division is to Amazon's operating economics relative to its share of revenue.

That is the pattern worth remembering. Amazon does not depend on a single way of monetizing activity. A shopper can create retail revenue. A seller can create service revenue. The search for a product can create advertising revenue. A membership can create subscription revenue. And businesses far removed from shopping can generate AWS revenue by renting Amazon's computing infrastructure. The power of the model is not that one of these businesses secretly replaces all the others. It is that several large businesses occupy different layers of the same corporate machine.
